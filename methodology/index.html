How the Density Score works.

Every produce item in PLUed carries a score from 0 to 99 that says how nutrient-rich it is, compared with other produce. This page is the whole recipe: the data, the math, and the published research we tested it against.

Read this first: this scale starts at healthy

The Density Score compares produce with produce. Every item on this scale is a whole food from the fresh aisle. A 30 here is a fresh fruit or vegetable with water, fiber, and vitamins in it. It is a stronger everyday choice than nearly anything sold in a wrapper.

Apps that score packaged food, such as Yuka or Nutri-Score, rate a different universe. Their low scores flag additives, added sugar, and heavy processing. Produce has none of those, which is exactly why those systems give almost all fresh produce the same top grade and stop being useful in the produce aisle. Our score picks up where theirs run out of resolution: it ranks the aisle itself, so you can see that a bowl of raspberries carries more nutrition than a bowl of iceberg.

So a PLUed 40 and a packaged-food 40 are different animals. Ours means "a solid everyday fruit or vegetable, with denser picks available on the same shelf." It never means "avoid this." There is no red on this scale.

The score: four questions, one number

The Density Score is a weighted blend of four measurements, each answering a question a shopper actually has.

  • Quality, 50% of the score. What is this food made of once you set the water aside? We total the fiber and eight vitamins and minerals (potassium, calcium, magnesium, vitamins A, C, E, K, and folate) as a share of their FDA Daily Values, per gram of dry matter. Sugar then reduces the result in proportion to how much of that dry matter it is. Grapes are mostly sugar once the water is gone, and the math says so.
  • Payload, 30% of the score. What does a real serving deliver? The same Daily Value totals, measured for the FDA reference serving of that food (for example 85 g of leafy greens, 140 g of fruit). This axis is why raspberries outrank iceberg: a serving of berries is food, a serving of iceberg is mostly crunch.
  • Lightness, 10% of the score. How many calories per 100 g as eaten, water included? Waterier foods score higher here. It is a small tiebreaker that honors "eat freely" foods without letting water alone win.
  • Phyto, 10% of the score. The compounds beyond vitamins: anthocyanins in blueberries, lycopene in tomatoes and watermelon, glucosinolates in broccoli and Brussels sprouts, the sulfur compounds of garlic. We measure six bioactive families from USDA content databases (flavonoids, proanthocyanidins, carotenoids, glucosinolates, isoflavones, and allium organosulfur compounds) and credit each food for its richest family. No Daily Value exists for these compounds, so this axis is scaled against the produce aisle itself and capped at a tenth of the score.

Water is deliberately counted on both sides: removed in Quality so filler cannot flatter a food's makeup, and included in Lightness because dilution is precisely what makes a food light. Tap any score in the app to see that item's own three sub-scores, its water share, its sugar share, and the full nutrient panel behind them.

The data: USDA, item by item

Nutrient values come from the USDA's laboratory food-composition database (SR Legacy, public domain). Each of PLUed's 876 produce items is matched to its USDA food, and the whole mapping was verified four separate ways: an independent recomputation of every score, a row-by-row review of all 872 mappings by five independent reviewers, external checks of every stand-in against published nutrition data, and a comparison against the FDA's own retail produce nutrition program values for the 40 most common fruits and vegetables.

A small number of specialty items have no USDA entry of their own. Those are scored as their closest USDA relative and labeled that way in the app ("scored as: Grapes"), so nothing is presented as more precise than it is. A handful of items that are not food in practice, such as ornamental gourds and decorative corn, carry no score at all.

Serving sizes use the FDA's reference amounts customarily consumed (RACC), the same standard behind Nutrition Facts labels, rather than whatever cup measure happens to appear in a database.

The bands

  • 75 to 99: Powerhouse. The leafy greens and their neighbors. Watercress, spinach, kale, chard.
  • 50 to 74: Strong. Broccoli, berries, peppers, sweet potato.
  • 25 to 49: Everyday. Apples, bananas, grapes, potatoes. Solid daily food.
  • Under 25: Treat. The fruit-stand candy, like fresh dates. Still fruit. Still fine.

Notice the floor: the lowest band is called a treat, because that is what it is. Nothing in the produce aisle earns a warning label from us.

The evidence: tested against the published research

Before shipping, we correlated the Density Score against the established academic measures of nutrient density, computed from the same USDA data:

  • 0.89 rank correlation with the CDC-published "Powerhouse Fruits and Vegetables" nutrient-density ranking (Di Noia, Preventing Chronic Disease, 2014), the only peer-reviewed ranking built specifically for produce.
  • 0.93 rank correlation with the Nutrient Rich Foods index (NRF9.3, Drewnowski), the validated measure behind most front-of-pack nutrition scoring, computed exactly across our full catalog.
  • 0.97 rank correlation when our nutrient panel is run through the CDC study's own per-calorie method, showing our nine-nutrient basket stands in for their seventeen almost perfectly.

The Phyto axis follows the same path the research is taking: the NRF's own author added flavonoids to fruit scoring in 2020 (NRF9f.3), and the USDA published its first glucosinolate database in 2026. Our score simply carries that direction across all six measurable families.

Where we knowingly differ from those indices, it is the serving-size question. Per-calorie scoring quietly assumes you eat 100 calories of everything, which flunks raspberries and blueberries as "not dense" while a real serving of strawberries delivers most of a day's vitamin C for about 45 calories. Our Payload axis measures the serving you actually eat. That is the main place our ranking and the per-calorie literature part ways, and we consider it a correction rather than a disagreement.

References: Di Noia J., Defining Powerhouse Fruits and Vegetables, Prev Chronic Dis 2014;11:130390 · Drewnowski A., The Nutrient Rich Foods index, Am J Clin Nutr 2010 · USDA FoodData Central · FDA nutrition information for raw fruits and vegetables

Honest limits

  • Bioactive compounds have no Daily Values. The Phyto axis uses measured content from USDA databases, never the withdrawn "antioxidant capacity" (ORAC) numbers, and is scaled against the produce aisle itself because no regulatory reference exists. Garlic-family sulfur compounds come from published laboratory studies rather than a USDA database, since those compounds only form when the food is cut or crushed. This is why the axis is capped at a tenth of the score.
  • The score is informational, never medical. It ranks nutrient density. It does not know your diet, your needs, or your doctor's advice, and it makes no health claims.
  • USDA values are averages. A specific melon's nutrition varies with variety, soil, season, and ripeness. The score describes the food, and no single piece of fruit signed a contract.
  • Weights are choices. The 55/35/10 blend and the sugar penalty are editorial decisions, published here so you can judge them. The correlations above show the outcome stays firmly anchored to the peer-reviewed consensus.
